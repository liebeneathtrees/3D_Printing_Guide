1 / 12 — Introduction

Welcome to the guide

A reference for 3D printing across brands, firmware, and slicers — built for breadth rather than one specific setup.

Most 3D printing guides fall into one of two traps: they're either written for one exact printer and stop being useful the moment you own something else, or they're so general they never tell you anything concrete enough to act on. This guide tries to split the difference. It covers the concepts that hold true across machines — how calibration works, what a slicer setting actually does, how firmware families differ — while still naming names: specific printers, specific slicers, specific settings.

If you're looking for a guide built around one exact printer and firmware combination instead, this isn't it — but a companion guide exists for exactly that use case, built the same way, just narrower and deeper.

Who this is for

Anyone past the total-beginner stage who owns — or is choosing between — a current mainstream FDM printer, and wants to understand the landscape rather than just follow one manufacturer's onboarding wizard. That includes people switching slicers, people comparing printers before a purchase, and people who inherited a printer from someone else and are trying to get oriented.

How this guide is organized

The sidebar moves from broad orientation (what printers and slicers exist, how firmware differs) into the concepts that apply no matter what you own (calibration, key settings, file formats), then into day-to-day reference material (troubleshooting, glossary). Every section stands on its own — jump straight to Troubleshooting if a print just failed, or Slicer Landscape if you're mid-decision on what software to use.

// TIP

Where this guide discusses a specific brand or slicer, it's covering the current mainstream flagship lineups as of 2026 — not every model ever made. Section 2 and 3 name exactly which ones.

A note on this being a moving target

Printer lineups, slicer features, and firmware capabilities are all under active development. Specs, pricing, and exact menu wording will drift over time even where the underlying concepts hold steady — treat the comparison charts in particular as a snapshot, not a permanent ranking.

Printer landscape

Five brands, current flagship lineups — where each one fits and who tends to pick it.

Every brand below is FDM (fused deposition modeling — melted plastic filament, laid down layer by layer). Resin printers are a different technology with their own workflow and aren't covered here. Within FDM, the two frame types you'll see repeated throughout this guide are bedslingers (the bed itself moves front-to-back, like the classic Ender 3) and CoreXY (the bed only moves up and down, while the print head moves in the horizontal plane) — CoreXY machines are typically faster and better suited to a fully enclosed chamber.

Slicer landscape

Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer, Cura, and Creality Print — how they compare and where each one fits.

Four of the five slicers in this guide share a surprising amount of DNA. PrusaSlicer forked from the open-source Slic3r project in 2017; Bambu Studio forked from PrusaSlicer in 2022 with Bambu-specific additions; OrcaSlicer forked from Bambu Studio in 2023 to strip out the cloud dependency and broaden printer support; and the current Creality Print is itself forked from OrcaSlicer. Only Cura, built independently by Ultimaker, sits outside that family tree. That shared lineage is good news in practice — the interface paradigm and a lot of underlying settings carry over between them, which is most of why Section 6 (Migrating Between Slicers) is shorter than you'd expect.

// WORTH KNOWING

Bambu Studio is licensed AGPLv3, and the Software Freedom Conservancy opened a formal investigation in 2026 into whether Bambu Lab has withheld networking-component source code required under that license. It doesn't change how the slicer prints today, but it's relevant if you care about the ecosystem staying forkable the way OrcaSlicer was able to fork it in 2023.

Bambu Studio

The slicer every Bambu Lab printer ships with, and the default choice if you own one and want zero friction. Its strength is depth of integration rather than breadth: automatic AMS filament detection via RFID, Lidar-assisted first-layer scanning tied directly to the printer, and a color-painting tool for multi-material models that nothing else in this guide matches. The trade-off is that it's tuned for Bambu hardware specifically — it will slice for other printers, but that's not what it's built for.

OrcaSlicer

The closest thing to a "universal" slicer in this list, and the one this guide leans on most when a recommendation has to work across brands. It ships with built-in profiles for 70+ printer families — Bambu, Prusa, Creality, Voron, Elegoo, Anycubic, and many community builds — and nine calibration wizards (temperature towers, flow rate, pressure advance, and more) accessible from the menu bar, rather than requiring separate downloaded test files. If you own printers from more than one manufacturer, OrcaSlicer is the only slicer here that comfortably manages all of them in one application. It's also the strongest Klipper integration of the five, which is why it comes up repeatedly in the Migrating and Calibration sections.

PrusaSlicer

The original codebase this whole family descended from, and still the manufacturer-native choice for Prusa printers. Its organic supports (introduced in version 2.6) are a genuine standout feature — cleaner branch generation than most alternatives. Where it falls behind OrcaSlicer is raw printer compatibility and some of the newer speed-oriented features like per-object acceleration control; where it falls behind Cura is sheer breadth of out-of-the-box profiles for non-Prusa hardware.

Cura

Historically the most widely used slicer of any on this list, and still the one with the largest built-in profile library — over 300 machine profiles, plus a plugin marketplace for anything missing. For an older bedslinger, or a printer running stock Marlin rather than Klipper, Cura is often still the fastest path to a working first print. Its position has slipped in 2025–2026 comparisons, though, on slower feature development relative to OrcaSlicer and PrusaSlicer, and a marketplace/"Cura Enterprise" direction some of the community has been lukewarm on.

Creality Print

Worth a direct correction here: current Creality Print is not a reskinned Cura, even though that description was accurate years ago and still circulates. The actively developed version ships factory-tuned profiles for Creality's fleet, full Klipper support for the K-series, and — because it's forked from OrcaSlicer — a genuinely modern slicing engine underneath. (The old Cura-based tool still exists as "Creality Slicer," kept around mainly for older 8-bit Marlin boards; it's a different, legacy product.) If you own a Creality printer, Creality Print is the path of least resistance for a properly calibrated first print, especially anything using Creality's CFS multicolor system.

// MENTIONED, NOT COVERED

Anycubic Slicer Next and Elegoo Slicer both exist as manufacturer-branded slicers, and both are themselves OrcaSlicer forks with brand-specific printer profiles baked in. They're not covered as separate profiles in this guide because the underlying workflow is functionally identical to OrcaSlicer — if you own an Anycubic or Elegoo printer, everything this guide says about OrcaSlicer applies to them too.

Firmware concepts

Klipper vs. Marlin vs. proprietary firmware, explained generically — what actually differs, and why it matters for the printer you own or are choosing.

Firmware is the software running on your printer's control board — it's what turns G-code instructions from the slicer into actual motor pulses, heater PID loops, and sensor readings. Every printer in this guide runs one of three broad firmware families, and which one it runs shapes a surprising amount of what you'll read elsewhere in this guide: how calibration works (Section 5), what a tablet or host device is doing (if anything), and how much you can modify the printer's behavior yourself.

Marlin: everything on one board

Marlin runs entirely on the printer's own control board — an 8-bit or 32-bit microcontroller with limited memory and processing power. Every motion calculation, heater loop, and stepper pulse happens on that one chip. This is the older, more established approach: it's open-source, supports an enormous range of hardware going back over a decade, and doesn't require anything beyond the printer itself — no companion computer, no network setup. The trade-off is a processing ceiling. Marlin's newer versions (2.1+) added Linear Advance and basic input shaping and can match Klipper's quality at moderate speeds, but past roughly 100mm/s the limited onboard compute becomes the bottleneck.

Klipper: the work split in two

Klipper takes a fundamentally different architecture: it splits the workload across two processors. A separate, more powerful computer — typically a Raspberry Pi or similar single-board computer, sometimes built into a tablet or console — handles the heavy math: trajectory planning, input shaping, and the fine timing that produces smooth curves. The printer's own board is reduced to a "dumb" pulse generator, executing pre-calculated stepper commands in real time. That split is why Klipper printers can hit meaningfully higher stepping rates and speeds than Marlin on equivalent hardware, and why features like input shaping (canceling out resonance/ringing using an accelerometer) and pressure advance (Klipper's more precise take on Linear Advance) are associated with Klipper specifically — they lean on compute that a bare microcontroller doesn't have. The cost is setup complexity: you need a host device, a network connection, and Klipper's configuration lives in a text file rather than a menu system, which is more powerful but less immediately approachable.

// TIP

Klipper's real day-to-day advantage for most people isn't raw speed — it's the web interface. Mainsail and Fluidd (the two common Klipper front ends) run in a browser on any device on your network, replacing a printer's small onboard screen with live temperature graphs, a webcam feed, and full manual control.

Proprietary firmware: manufacturer-controlled

The third category isn't really one firmware — it's a pattern several manufacturers follow: take an open-source base (usually Marlin or Klipper) and heavily customize it, often keeping some components closed even where the license requires the rest to stay open. Bambu Lab's printers run a modified version of Klipper, tightly integrated with Bambu Studio and the AMS system. Prusa's printers run "Buddy" firmware, a heavily customized Marlin derivative with Prusa-specific additions like load-cell-based auto leveling. In both cases, the manufacturer trades some of the openness and modifiability of stock Marlin or Klipper for tighter integration and more consistent out-of-box tuning — the calibration steps in Section 5 are partly automated away on these machines precisely because the manufacturer controls the whole stack.

5 / 12 — Calibration & slicing

Calibration concepts

What PID tuning, e-steps, flow rate, and pressure advance actually are — universally — with a quick-reference table for how each is handled per firmware family.

Every printer, regardless of brand or firmware, benefits from the same handful of calibration steps. What differs is how you run them: some are a console command, some are a slicer-guided test print, and on tightly integrated proprietary machines, some happen automatically before you ever think about them. This section explains what each calibration step is actually doing; Section 7 (Key Slicer Settings) and your printer's own documentation cover the exact commands for your specific setup.

PID tuning

PID tuning teaches the firmware exactly how your specific hotend and bed heat up and hold temperature, instead of using generic assumed values. Without it, temperatures tend to overshoot and oscillate around the target rather than holding steady. This is a one-time-per-hardware-change calibration — redo it after swapping a hotend or heater cartridge, not before every print.

E-steps (extruder calibration)

Confirms the extruder motor moves exactly as much filament as it's told to. The classic test: mark filament a fixed distance from the extruder's entry point, command it to extrude 100mm, then measure what's actually left. If the number is off, the steps-per-mm value in the firmware needs a proportional adjustment. This is usually accurate on factory-calibrated machines and mainly worth re-checking after an extruder upgrade or swap.

Flow rate / extrusion multiplier

Fine-tunes how much plastic actually comes out relative to what the slicer commands, correcting for small real-world variance between filament brands, colors, and even spools. This one lives at the slicer level rather than the firmware level on most setups — OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio both include a guided flow-rate calibration test (Section 7), which is usually faster and more reliable than doing it by hand.

Pressure advance / linear advance

When the extruder speeds up or slows down, pressure builds and releases in the molten filament with a slight delay, causing blobby corners and inconsistent extrusion at direction changes. This calibration predicts that delay and compensates for it in real time. It goes by different names depending on firmware — pressure advance on Klipper, linear advance on Marlin — and Klipper's implementation is generally considered more precise, one of the concrete quality differences firmware makes. Slicer-guided test patterns (a small tower or line printed across a range of values) make this fast to dial in visually rather than guessing.

Input shaping (Klipper-specific, hardware-dependent)

If an accelerometer is present — built in on some machines, an optional add-on on others — the firmware can measure the printer's resonant frequencies and automatically reduce ringing and ghosting at higher speeds. This is a Klipper feature specifically; Marlin's 2.1+ input shaping exists but is less capable, and proprietary firmware varies by manufacturer (Bambu and Prusa both handle resonance compensation internally, calibrated at the factory).

Acceleration and velocity limits

Every firmware sets ceilings on how fast the printer is allowed to move and how quickly it can change speed. Stock values are conservative and safe for an untuned machine. Once bed leveling and (where applicable) input shaping are dialed in, these limits can usually be raised incrementally — small jumps, followed by a test print with fine detail and sharp corners, watching for ringing or missed steps creeping back in. There's no universal "right" number; it's specific to your frame's rigidity and whatever resonance testing found.

Retraction tuning

Controls stringing between separate parts of a print. This is tuned per-filament rather than per-printer, and every major slicer in Section 3 has some form of built-in retraction test tower to speed it up.

Migrating between slicers

Universal gotchas when switching slicers, two detailed step-by-step walkthroughs for the most common migrations, and quick tips for other specific pairs.

Because most of the slicers in Section 3 share a common ancestor, switching between them is far less painful than switching printer brands. But "less painful" isn't "seamless" — a handful of gotchas show up regardless of which two slicers you're moving between, and the exact steps differ depending on where you're headed.

Universal rules, regardless of which two slicers

  • Profiles never transfer automatically

    No two of these slicers share a native profile format, even the ones forked from each other. Write down your dialed-in temps, speeds, retraction settings, and cooling tweaks per filament before switching — you're manually re-entering them as a starting point, not importing a file. (The 3MF project inspector in Section 10 can help extract these numbers from a downloaded model, which is a related but different problem.)

  • Always re-verify temperatures and retraction on the new slicer

    Even a "close enough" number carried over from another slicer is worth re-testing rather than trusting blindly — small differences in how each slicer calculates retraction distance or reports temperature can produce a working print in one and stringing or blobbing in another.

  • Check start/end G-code against firmware macros

    If your printer's firmware already defines a startup routine (a Klipper START_PRINT macro is the classic example, but Marlin setups can have equivalents), the new slicer's default start G-code should call that routine rather than duplicating homing, leveling, and priming commands itself. Duplicated logic here is the single most common cause of a blob or pause right at print start after any slicer switch.

  • Reconnect network printing separately from installing the slicer

    Sending files directly to the printer over your network (rather than USB/SD) requires its own connection setup in the new slicer — an IP address plus, on Klipper machines, a Moonraker API key, or the equivalent cloud login on proprietary ecosystems. This is a separate step from installing the software and is easy to forget.

  • Re-run calibration tests in the new slicer before trusting results

    Flow rate and pressure/linear advance values are somewhat slicer-specific in how they're expressed and tested. If the new slicer has built-in guided calibration (most of the ones in Section 3 do), run flow rate, then pressure advance, then retraction, in that order — see Section 5 for what each one does.

  • Do a side-by-side first print

    Slice the same model in both slicers once and compare. It's the fastest way to build confidence that the migration preserved — or improved on — your existing quality, and it catches profile mistakes before a long print wastes filament.

    • !! WATCH FOR

    A blob or pause at the very start of a print, immediately after switching slicers, is almost always leftover priming/purge G-code in the new slicer's default start script conflicting with a firmware macro that already primes the nozzle. Check the start G-code step above first.

    Step-by-step: migrating to OrcaSlicer (from any other slicer)

    This is the single most common migration covered in this guide — OrcaSlicer's calibration tools and cross-brand profile support (Section 3) make it the default "graduation" slicer regardless of which brand you started on.

  • Write down your current settings

    In your existing slicer, note your dialed-in nozzle/bed temps, print speed, retraction distance and speed, and any cooling fan tweaks, per filament you actually use. You won't reuse these blindly, but they're a known-good starting point instead of factory defaults.

  • Install OrcaSlicer

    Download it from the official OrcaSlicer GitHub releases page and install like any other application. It can run alongside your current slicer — no need to uninstall anything yet.

  • Add your printer profile

    On first launch, use the setup wizard and search for your exact printer model in the list — OrcaSlicer's built-in library covers 70+ printer families (Section 3), so most current machines from the brands in Section 2 are already there with correct bed size and kinematics. If yours genuinely isn't listed, a close same-brand model or a community profile is the fallback.

  • Connect OrcaSlicer to the printer over your network

    The exact steps depend on your printer's firmware (Section 4). On a Klipper machine, look for a device/connection tab and enter the host's IP address plus the Moonraker API key, found in Mainsail or Fluidd under Settings → Moonraker → API Key. On a Bambu Lab printer, OrcaSlicer supports both cloud mode (same login as Bambu Studio) and LAN-only mode (requires enabling Developer Mode + LAN Only on the printer first, then entering its IP and access code). Other proprietary printers vary — check whether the manufacturer publishes a direct-connection or "3rd-party slicer" guide (Creality does, for example) before assuming it isn't supported.

  • Check start/end G-code against your printer's macros

    Same principle as the universal rule above, applied concretely: if a Klipper START_PRINT macro (or equivalent) already handles homing, bed mesh, and priming, OrcaSlicer's default start G-code should call that macro rather than duplicating the steps itself.

  • Carry over your working temps and speeds

    Plug in the numbers from step 1 as your starting filament profile in OrcaSlicer. There's no automatic import — your old slicer and OrcaSlicer don't share a profile format, so this is manual re-entry, not a file conversion. The numbers themselves will likely need small nudges, not a full redo.

  • Run OrcaSlicer's calibration tests

    Under the Calibration menu: run flow rate, then pressure advance, then a retraction test, in that order, per filament type you use regularly. Save the results into each filament profile — this is the main payoff for switching. See Section 5 for why each of these matters.

  • Do a side-by-side first print

    Slice the same model in both slicers once, just to compare. Fastest way to build confidence the migration preserved — or improved on — your existing quality.

    Step-by-step: migrating to a manufacturer's native slicer

    Common after buying new hardware from a different brand — say, moving from a Klipper-based printer to a Bambu Lab or Prusa machine — where the native slicer is the path of least resistance for the new printer's factory-tuned profiles and any hardware-specific features (AMS, MMU, Lidar scanning).

  • Write down your current settings, but expect to lean on them less

    Same principle as above, but with lower confidence this time: a genuinely different printer (new nozzle, new hotend, new frame rigidity) means your old numbers are a rough starting point at best, not a close match.

  • Install the native slicer and create an account if required

    Bambu Studio and Creality Print both expect a cloud account for full functionality (remote monitoring, cloud printing); PrusaSlicer does not. If you'd rather not use the cloud features, most of these slicers still function locally — check Section 4 and the manufacturer's own docs for LAN-only setup.

  • Select the exact printer model and nozzle size

    Native slicers are built around exact hardware matches — unlike OrcaSlicer's broad compatibility, picking the wrong model here (or the wrong nozzle diameter) produces meaningfully wrong first-layer height and flow calculations, not just a minor mismatch.

  • Start from the manufacturer's filament profile, not your old numbers

    For the specific filament brands the manufacturer has factory-tuned (Bambu's own PLA/PETG lines, for example), their pre-built profile is usually a better starting point than manually re-entering your old slicer's numbers. For third-party filament, use your old numbers as a reference and expect to run the manufacturer slicer's own calibration tools if it has them.

  • Check whether hardware-specific features need separate setup

    AMS/CFS/ACE multi-material systems, Lidar-based first-layer scanning, and similar features are configured per-slicer and won't carry over any state from your old setup — treat them as a first-time setup even if you've used similar systems on another brand.

  • Run a calibration/test print before anything important

    Most native slicers ship with some form of guided calibration (Bambu Studio and current Creality Print both do). Run it before trusting the printer with anything beyond a quick test object — factory tuning covers the printer in general, not your specific filament and environment.

    7 / 12 — Calibration & slicing

    Key slicer settings

    The handful of settings that actually decide how a print looks, how strong it is, and how long it takes — explained once, referenced everywhere.

    Every slicer in Section 3 buries these in slightly different menus, but the settings themselves are universal across brands and firmware. Understanding what each one trades off is worth more than any specific number.

    Layer height

    How thick each printed layer is, measured in millimeters. It's the single biggest lever for the trade-off between quality and print time.

    Layer heightResultGood for
    0.12–0.16mmFine detail, smooth curves, longer print timeMiniatures, display pieces, anything with visible curves
    0.20mmThe standard default — balanced quality and speedMost everyday prints
    0.28–0.32mmVisible layer lines, noticeably fasterFunctional parts, prototypes, large plain objects

    A standard 0.4mm nozzle — the default on nearly every printer in this guide — prints reliably from about 0.08mm up to 0.32mm layer height, regardless of brand. Going much beyond that range starts to fight the nozzle geometry itself rather than any particular printer's limits.

    Wall count (perimeters)

    The number of solid outline loops printed around the outside of each layer, before infill starts. This is the main driver of strength and watertightness — more so than infill, for most parts.

    • 2 walls — thin, mostly cosmetic strength. Fine for display models.
    • 3 walls (a common default) — solid all-around choice for general use.
    • 4–6 walls — meaningfully stronger, worth it for functional parts that take load or stress, at the cost of print time and material.

    Infill percentage and pattern

    The internal lattice structure inside a print, hidden under the walls and top layers.

    • 0–10% — decorative or display-only prints where weight and speed matter more than strength.
    • 15–20% (a common default) — reasonable strength for most general-purpose prints.
    • 40%+ — functional parts under real mechanical load. Beyond roughly 50%, adding more walls or top/bottom layers usually strengthens a part more efficiently than pushing infill higher.

    Pattern matters too: grid and gyroid are common general-purpose defaults; gyroid in particular prints quietly and is strong in all directions, making it a good default to leave alone unless there's a specific reason to change it.

    Print speed

    Faster isn't free — it's a trade against surface quality and reliability, and how much speed a given printer can absorb without losing quality depends heavily on frame rigidity and calibration (Section 5). A well-tuned machine with pressure advance and input shaping dialed in can run meaningfully faster than a stock, uncalibrated one at the same settings — this is a big part of why identically priced printers from different brands can feel very different in practice.

    Supports

    Sacrificial structures printed under overhanging geometry that would otherwise droop or fail mid-print, removed after printing.

    • When they're needed: as a rule of thumb, overhangs steeper than about 45° from vertical typically need support underneath them.
    • Normal (grid) supports — simpler, easier to remove cleanly from flat-ish surfaces, more prone to leaving marks on the model surface where they touch.
    • Tree/organic supports — branch outward from fewer contact points, often leaving a cleaner surface and using less material, especially on organic or curved models. PrusaSlicer's organic supports and OrcaSlicer's tree supports (Section 3) are both strong implementations of this idea.
    • Support interface / support blocker — a thin, denser layer between support and model that makes removal cleaner; worth enabling by default once discovered.

    Top/bottom layers and shells

    How many fully solid layers print on the top and bottom surfaces before infill takes over. Too few and infill pattern "ghosts" through the top surface, or the bottom layer sags into gaps; 4–5 layers (roughly 0.8–1mm total, depending on layer height) is a reasonable default for most prints.

    // TIP

    When a print isn't turning out the way you want, change one of these settings at a time and note the result. Layer height, wall count, and infill interact with each other — chasing all three at once makes it hard to tell what actually fixed (or broke) anything.

    // SEE ALSO

    These settings are universal, but the calibration values that make them print cleanly — flow rate, pressure advance, retraction — are covered in and tuned per-filament in .

    File types & workflow

    Three file types, three jobs. Knowing which one you're holding avoids a lot of confusion.

    FormatWhat it isWhen you touch it
    .STLRaw 3D geometry — just the shape, no printer settings, no color/material info.Downloaded models, exported from CAD software, before slicing.
    .3MFA project format that bundles geometry with slicer settings, plate arrangement, supports, and — in Bambu Studio/OrcaSlicer-lineage slicers — multi-part or multi-color setup.Saving/reopening a project you want to reprint or tweak later with all settings intact.
    .gcodeThe final, sliced machine instructions — exact toolpaths, temperatures, and speeds, specific to the settings and printer used to generate it.The file actually sent to the printer to print.
    // A NOTE ON .3MF PORTABILITY

    A .3MF from one slicer family doesn't always open cleanly in another — Bambu Studio/OrcaSlicer-lineage slicers (Section 3) use a shared settings schema inside the file, while Cura and PrusaSlicer each structure their .3MF project data differently again from each other. The geometry always transfers; the embedded print settings don't automatically carry over between families. Section 10's project file inspector reads all three schemas and will tell you which one it detected.

    Where models come from

    If you're not designing your own, a few sites cover most needs: Printables and Thingiverse are the largest general-purpose repositories with huge free libraries; MakerWorld is newer, growing fast, and often has print-profile metadata bundled directly into the download. All let you filter by printer or bed size — worth double-checking a model's listed dimensions against your own printer's build volume (Section 2) before committing to a long print, especially on the smaller end of the range covered in this guide.

    When a model needs repair

    Occasionally an STL is "non-manifold" — it has small gaps, flipped faces, or overlapping geometry that confuses the slicer, usually showing up as odd holes or a failed slice. Every slicer in Section 3 includes a built-in repair tool (often labeled "Fix model" or similar) that automatically patches most of these issues — worth trying before troubleshooting anything else if a model won't slice cleanly or the preview looks broken.

    // TIP

    If an imported model looks impossibly tiny or absurdly huge, it's almost always a units mismatch — the model was designed in inches and imported as millimeters, or vice versa. Most slicers offer a quick scale-by-factor option (25.4x either direction) to fix this without re-exporting.

    The typical workflow

    1. Get a model as an .STL (or sometimes .STEP/.OBJ from CAD sources) from a model repository or your own design software.
    2. Open it in your slicer, position and orient it, apply supports if needed, and set your filament/print profile.
    3. Optional but recommended: save the arrangement as a .3MF project file if it's something you'll print again or want to revisit — this preserves everything, not just the shape.
    4. Slice it, producing a .gcode file.
    5. Send the G-code to the printer — over your network directly (fastest, and the norm on Klipper and most current proprietary machines), or via USB/SD card as a fallback if the network connection is down.
    6. Start the print from whatever your printer uses as its control interface — a touchscreen, an app, or a browser-based panel.
    // TIP

    Get in the habit of saving a .3MF alongside the .gcode for anything you might print more than once. Re-slicing from a bare .STL later means redoing supports and placement from scratch; reopening a .3MF restores everything instantly.

    Organizing project files

    A simple folder structure avoids a lot of "which settings did I use for this?" confusion later:

    • Keep source .STL files separate from sliced output.
    • Save the .3MF project next to (or instead of) the raw STL once you've dialed in a print.
    • Name G-code files with the material and date if you experiment with settings often — e.g. bracket_petg_v2.gcode — so your printer's file list stays meaningful weeks later.

    Filament care & storage

    Humidity is the quiet cause behind a large share of "mystery" print failures, on any printer. This section is about preventing that before it starts.

    Why humidity is the enemy

    Most 3D printing filaments are hygroscopic — they pull moisture out of the air over time, even indoors. Once that moisture is absorbed into the plastic, it turns to steam the instant it hits the hotend, and that steam has to go somewhere. The result shows up as print defects that look unrelated to filament at first glance:

    • Popping or crackling sounds while printing — the clearest audible sign.
    • Stringing and oozing that gets worse over time even with retraction correctly tuned.
    • Rough, matte, or bubbly surface texture instead of a smooth finish.
    • Weak layer adhesion — parts that snap or delaminate more easily than they should.
    • Inconsistent extrusion that looks like an under-extrusion problem but isn't.
    // SEE ALSO

    If you're troubleshooting any of the symptoms above, check — but if a spool has been open for a while, come back here first.

    Material sensitivity, ranked

    Not all filaments absorb moisture at the same rate — worth knowing so you don't over-invest in protecting PLA or under-invest in protecting TPU.

    • Most sensitive: TPU, nylon — absorb moisture fast, sometimes within a day or two of open-air exposure in humid conditions.
    • Moderately sensitive: PETG — noticeably more sensitive than PLA, especially prone to visible popping when wet.
    • Least sensitive (but not immune): PLA, ABS — more forgiving, can sit out longer, but will still degrade over weeks to months.

    Storage methods

    MethodCostGood for
    Resealable bag + desiccantVery lowOccasional-use spools, short-term storage between prints
    Vacuum-seal bag + desiccantLowLonger-term storage, spools you won't touch for weeks
    Dry box (airtight container + desiccant)Low–ModerateEveryday spool storage between print sessions, easy to grab and go
    Active filament dryerModerateDrying out already-wet spools, printing moisture-sensitive materials (TPU, nylon, PETG) directly from a dry, heated box

    A reasonable setup for most printers: keep the spool currently loaded in a basic dry box or sealed container with desiccant at all times when not actively printing, and reserve a dedicated dryer (or drying cycles) for TPU, nylon, and any spool that's shown early signs of moisture pickup. A few printers in Section 2 — the Bambu AMS, Creality's CFS, Anycubic's ACE Pro — build active drying into their multi-material feeder units, which covers this automatically for spools loaded into them.

    Desiccant strategy

    • Silica gel with a color indicator is the easiest to manage — orange/blue beads turn green/pink (formulation-dependent) when saturated, giving a clear visual cue instead of guesswork.
    • How much: more surface area of desiccant beats a small packet — a wide, shallow tray or several bags spread around the spool works better than one small sachet.
    • Recharging silica gel: most reusable silica gel can be dried out in a home oven at a low temperature (roughly 100–120°C / 212–250°F) for 1–2 hours, spread thin on a tray, until it returns to its "dry" color. Let it cool before returning it to the container.
    • Replace vs. recharge: silica gel can be recharged many times; only replace it once it stops changing color reliably after a recharge cycle.

    Drying filament that's already wet

    If a spool is showing popping, stringing, or a rough finish and it's been stored loosely, dry it before troubleshooting anything else on the printer or in the slicer.

    // TIP

    Not sure if a spool is wet without printing a test? Snap a short offcut of filament in half. Wet filament tends to bend and give a dull, fibrous break; dry filament snaps cleanly and quickly. Not foolproof, but a fast first check.

    MaterialDry tempTypical time
    PLA40–45°C4–6 hrs
    PETG55–65°C4–6 hrs
    ABS55–65°C2–4 hrs
    TPU45–50°C6–8 hrs
    !! CAUTION

    A dedicated filament dryer or food dehydrator with an accurate thermostat is the safest tool for this. A home oven can work in a pinch for some materials but is much harder to hold at a precise low temperature, and overshooting can warp the spool itself or soften filament onto it — go low and check often if that's the only option available.

    Building a budget dry box

    A basic dry box doesn't need to be a purchased product. A clear plastic storage tub with a tight-sealing lid, a couple of desiccant packs or a wide tray of silica gel, and a small hole drilled for the filament to feed through (a bit of PTFE tubing in the hole keeps it from fraying) works well for day-to-day storage, and holds multiple spools if sized generously. It won't actively dry filament like a heated dryer, but it slows moisture pickup dramatically compared to an open spool on a shelf.

    A simple ongoing habit

    • Store the spool currently on the printer in a dry box or sealed bag with desiccant whenever it's not actively printing overnight or for more than a day.
    • Keep a hygrometer inside one storage container as a general gauge — under roughly 30% relative humidity is a reasonable target.
    • Check desiccant color before starting any print with PETG, TPU, or nylon.
    • If a spool sits untouched for more than a couple of weeks, assume it needs a quick dry cycle before the next print rather than finding out mid-print.

      // TIP

      Change one variable at a time. If a print fails, resist adjusting temperature, speed, and retraction all at once — you'll fix the symptom but lose the information about what actually caused it.

      Preventive maintenance

      A little regular upkeep prevents most of the failures above from happening in the first place. None of this is printer-specific beyond the exact tools involved.

      • Every few prints: wipe down the bed surface (isopropyl alcohol works well), check the filament path feeds smoothly, and clear oozed/charred plastic off the outside of the nozzle.
      • Monthly-ish: check belt tension, inspect wheel/carriage play, lightly lubricate lead screws, check wiring at flex points (hotend and bed cables), and re-check bed leveling if first layers have started looking inconsistent.
      • Nozzle cleaning: a cold pull (heat, let cool slightly, then pull filament through firmly) clears minor residue buildup without disassembly.
      • Nozzle replacement: brass nozzles wear down over hundreds of print-hours, especially with abrasive filaments — swap if extrusion width or first-layer consistency degrades without another obvious cause.
      • Heat creep / PTFE liner check (Bowden setups): the PTFE liner inside the heat break can degrade after months of use at higher temperatures, causing gradually worsening under-extrusion or clicking at the extruder that no amount of e-step or flow tuning fixes.
      • Firmware/software updates: update related components together rather than piecemeal — on Klipper, that means Klipper, Moonraker, and Mainsail/Fluidd as a set, since most post-update issues are a version mismatch between components rather than a bug in any one of them.

      Most maintenance items double as troubleshooting fixes. If a symptom above doesn't have an obvious cause, work through this checklist before assuming it's a settings problem.

      Glossary

      Quick definitions for terms used throughout this guide.

      Bedslinger
      A frame layout where the print bed itself moves front-to-back while the print head moves left-right and up-down. Simpler and cheaper than CoreXY, but generally slower at high quality.
      CoreXY
      A frame layout where the bed only moves vertically (Z) while the print head moves in the full horizontal plane, driven by a pair of belts working together. Typically faster and better suited to a fully enclosed chamber than a bedslinger.
      Enclosed vs. open frame
      An enclosed printer has walls/a chamber around the print area, holding in heat and blocking drafts — important for warp-prone materials like ABS and ASA. An open-frame printer has no enclosure; simpler to access and maintain, but more draft-sensitive.
      AMS (Automatic Material System)
      Bambu Lab's multi-spool feeder unit, enabling automatic filament switching for multi-color prints. Competing systems include Creality's CFS and Anycubic's ACE.
      MMU (Multi-Material Unit)
      Prusa's term for its multi-material/multi-color add-on system, used with compatible printers for multi-filament prints without a full toolhead swap.
      Toolchanger
      A multi-material approach (used by the Prusa XL) where the printer physically swaps between several independent toolheads, each pre-loaded with its own filament — avoids the purge waste of single-toolhead multi-material systems.
      MCU
      The printer's own control board — handles motors, heaters, and sensors in real time. Under Klipper, it relies on a separate host for path planning; under Marlin, it does that work itself.
      Host
      On a Klipper printer, the more powerful companion computer (commonly a Raspberry Pi) that runs the main Klipper software and handles motion planning.
      Klippy
      The name of the Klipper host-side process that does the actual motion planning, as distinct from the firmware running on the printer's own board.
      printer.cfg
      Klipper's main configuration file — steppers, thermistors, macros, and calibrated values all live here, typically edited through a web interface like Mainsail or Fluidd.
      Moonraker
      The API layer connecting a Klipper printer to web interfaces and slicers over the network.
      Mainsail / Fluidd
      The two most common browser-based control panels for Klipper printers — live temperature graphs, manual controls, and a print queue, in place of a stock screen.
      Bowden vs. direct drive
      Two extruder layouts. Bowden mounts the extruder motor on the frame with a PTFE tube feeding filament to the hotend; direct drive mounts the motor right at the hotend, improving retraction response at the cost of added toolhead mass.
      Heat creep
      Heat migrating up the hotend beyond where it should, softening filament too early and causing jams or under-extrusion — often linked to a degraded PTFE liner on Bowden setups.
      Z-offset
      The nozzle's calibrated distance from the bed at the "zero" height.
      Bed mesh / bed leveling
      A map of small variations across the bed's surface that the firmware compensates for during printing, generated automatically on nearly every current printer.
      PID tuning
      Calibrating how the firmware controls heater power to reach and hold a target temperature without overshooting or oscillating.
      E-steps
      The number of extruder motor steps needed to move filament exactly 1mm — calibrated so the printer extrudes exactly as much plastic as commanded.
      Pressure advance / linear advance
      A firmware feature that compensates for pressure buildup in the hotend during speed changes, reducing blobbing at corners. "Pressure advance" on Klipper, "linear advance" on Marlin.
      Input shaping
      A firmware feature (most capable on Klipper, requires an accelerometer such as an ADXL345) that reduces ringing/ghosting by compensating for the printer's resonant frequencies.
      Flow rate / extrusion multiplier
      A correction factor for how much filament is actually extruded relative to what's commanded.
      Retraction
      Pulling filament back slightly during travel moves to reduce oozing and stringing.
      Wall count (perimeters)
      The number of solid outline loops printed around a part's outside before infill starts — the main driver of a part's strength.
      Infill
      The internal lattice structure inside a print, hidden under the walls and top/bottom layers, expressed as a percentage of solid fill.
      Supports (tree/organic vs. normal)
      Sacrificial structures printed under steep overhangs to keep them from drooping, removed after printing. Tree/organic supports branch from fewer contact points and often leave a cleaner surface than grid-style normal supports.
      G-code
      The line-by-line machine instructions a slicer produces and the printer executes.
      .3MF
      A project file format bundling model geometry with slicer settings and layout — the internal schema differs between the Bambu/OrcaSlicer-lineage slicers, PrusaSlicer, and Cura (Section 8), which is why the same .3mf can look complete in one slicer and geometry-only in another.
      Hygroscopic
      Prone to absorbing moisture from the air — true of most 3D printing filaments to varying degrees.
      PLA / PETG / ABS / TPU
